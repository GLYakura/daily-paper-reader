(Dated: August 18, 2026) Radio detection of ultra-high energy particles ( ≳ 10 18 eV) depends on the propagation and re-flection of broadband radio pulses at natural media boundaries. We extend the spherical-wave (Sommerfeld–Weyl) treatment of reflection from a single homogeneous interface to stratified media by replacing the Fresnel coefficient of each plane wave component with the characteristic-matrix re-flection coefficient of the layered medium. The calculation retains the spherical geometry, with the layered coefficient evaluated in the tangent plane at each incident point. Removing the layer con-trast recovers the single-boundary result to machine precision, while comparison with the published spherical-surface calculation gives agreement better than 1 .1% at ten HiCal-2 elevation angles, with a mean deviation of 0 .6%. We apply the formalism to shallow firn stacks proposed as explanations for the anomalous-polarity ANITA events. For realistic firn contrasts, the layers change the reflected amplitude but do not reverse the pulse polarity over 150–850 MHz for the elevations considered. In a reference s-polarized two-layer model, a sign change of the reflection coefficient requires a buried refractive index of 

n2 ≃ 2.56, 2 .04, and 1 .79 at local elevation angles of 8 ◦, 15 ◦, and 25 ◦, respectively. We also test the specular factorization used in fast propagation calculations and find agreement with the full angular integral at the 0 .2% level for high-altitude balloon geometries. Sources close to the boundary require the full angular integral because the stationary-phase region becomes broad. Finally, the calculated reflected pulses reproduce the expected polarity inversion in 101 of 106 HiCal-1 direct/reflected pulse pairs. Because the medium enters through its complex refractive index, the framework applies to ice, lunar regolith, and conducting media. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

Radio detection of ultra-high energy (UHE) neutri-nos and cosmic rays, here referring to primaries above roughly 10 18 eV, relies on coherent radio emission from particle cascades [1–3]. For UHE cosmic rays (UHE-CRs), the air-shower radio emission is dominated by the geomagnetic mechanism [3–6] and can be detected af-ter reflection from the Antarctic ice surface. In-ice neu-trino interactions produce Askaryan emission [1, 2, 7, 8], which can be observed after refraction through the ice– air boundary. We focus here on the reflection of UHECR signals. The same boundary formalism gives the corre-sponding transmission coefficient, but transmission from in-medium sources is beyond the scope of this work. The Antarctic Impulsive Transient Antenna (ANITA) provides the main high-altitude data set for this reflection channel. At an altitude of about 37 km, ANITA viewed 

O(10 6) km 2 of Antarctic ice with dual-polarization an-tennas over roughly 200–1200 MHz [9]. ANITA ob-served UHECR air showers through their geomagnetic radio emission reflected from the ice surface [10, 11]. For the horizontally polarized component, reflection from the higher-index ice reverses the pulse polarity. The non-inverted polarity of a small number of below-horizon events therefore remains unexplained [12–14]. To inves-tigate the effect of a stratified spherical surface on the

reflected pulse polarity, we apply our calculation to the two steep ANITA-I/III events and the four near-horizon ANITA-IV events reported in Refs. [12–14]. Surface reflectivity is an important part of balloon-borne UHECR measurements, as it enters directly into the reconstructed primary energy. ANITA flew with the High-Altitude Calibration (HiCal) transmitters, which emitted calibration pulses that were recorded by the ANITA payload both directly and after reflection from the Antarctic surface. The reflected-to-direct power ra-tio, denoted by r/d , provides a measure of the surface power reflection coefficient for each direct-reflected (D-R) pulse pair. HiCal-1 flew with ANITA-3 [15], while HiCal-2 flew with ANITA-4 during the 2016–2017 aus-tral flight season and provided more than 2500 direct-reflected pulse pairs for surface-reflectivity studies [16, 17]. The spherical single-boundary calculation was de-veloped and validated using these data [17] and later re-fined in Refs. [18, 19]. We use both HiCal data sets here: the HiCal-2 reflectivity results to validate the spherical single-boundary limit against the published calculation of Ref. [17], and HiCal-1 direct/reflected pulse pairs to validate the reflected-pulse calculation in Sec. V D. The calculation is then extended to stratified media. The same boundary response is relevant to radio-neutrino detectors that view the ice from within. The Askaryan Radio Array (ARA) is an in-ice radio ar-ray at the South Pole consisting of five independent stations, with antennas deployed at depths of up to about 200 m [20]. Its event reconstruction and neutrino searches include both refracted and reflected propagation arXiv:2608.15989v1 [astro-ph.IM] 17 Aug 2026 2through the ice [21–23]. The fifth ARA station also in-cludes a low-threshold phased array for improved sen-sitivity to low signal-to-noise-ratio (SNR) signals [24]. Askaryan emission from cosmic ray showers in ice has also been observed with ARA [25]. The Payload for Ultrahigh Energy Observations (PUEO), the balloon-borne successor to ANITA, com-pleted its Antarctic flight in 2025–2026 and extends the balloon-borne approach with improved sensitivity through a phased-array trigger [26, 27]. An accurate treatment of the boundary response is therefore relevant to both balloon-borne and in-ice radio detectors, as well as to radio sounding of ice, lunar regolith, and other sub-surface media. The HiCal geometry is not well described by a flat, smooth surface. At shallow incidence angles, the region contributing coherently to the reflected field can extend over hundreds of meters, so both surface curvature and height variations across this region can affect the mea-sured signal [15, 17, 18]. The spherical single-boundary treatment of Refs. [17, 19] accounts for these effects by writing the source field as a Hertz potential and using the Sommerfeld–Weyl identity, Eq. (5), to decompose the spherical wave into plane wave components. Each component is reflected from the local tangent plane at its incidence angle α0, as shown in Fig. 1, with surface roughness included in the angular integral. Evaluating the calculation across the measured frequency band and transforming back to the time domain gives the simulated reflected pulse. This treatment reproduces the HiCal-2 reflectivity and was later extended to reflected pulse waveforms [18, 19]. Stratification is especially relevant for Antarctic firn and for proposed subsurface explanations of the ANITA anomalous-polarity events. Antarctic firn has a radio-frequency refractive index that rises from about 1 .3 near the surface toward the deep-ice value n ≃ 1.78; in the South Pole parameterization used in ARA analyses, the near-surface index deficit has an e-folding depth of about 50 m [22, 23]. Smooth firn profiles are used in ray-tracing reconstructions, while discrete density contrasts from wind crusts, ice lenses, refrozen melt layers, and buried surfaces can add internally reflected components. Such structures have been proposed as possible con-tributors to the non-inverted polarity of several ANITA events [28], and subsequent ANITA and HiCal measure-ments have constrained these scenarios [29]. Here we ex-tend the spherical single-boundary calculation to a strat-ified medium by replacing the Fresnel coefficient of each plane wave component with the characteristic-matrix re-flection coefficient of the layered medium. This coefficient includes the coherent sum of the direct surface reflection and all contributions involving internal reflections and transmission through the layers. The spherical geom-etry, local angle construction, surface roughness treat-ment, and frequency-domain construction of broadband fields are otherwise unchanged. The characteristic-matrix treatment follows directly from the Maxwell boundary conditions and reduces to the single-boundary Fresnel coefficient when the layer con-trast is removed [30]. The same formulation applies to other stratified media, including lunar regolith, planetary ice, and lossy or conducting subsurface layers [31–34]. For the shallow firn stacks considered here, realistic firn contrasts change the reflected amplitude and phase but do not reverse the pulse polarity. The buried-layer in-dex required for a reversal depends strongly on the local elevation angle, approaching dense-ice values at higher elevations but rising well above measured firn and dense-ice values near the horizon. The resulting constraint on subsurface explanations of ANITA anomalous-polarity events is therefore elevation dependent, rather than set by a single material threshold. This paper has three main results. First, we de-velop a spherical-wave forward model for reflection from stratified media and show that it reduces to the vali-dated single-boundary calculation at machine precision. Second, we determine the elevation-dependent index re-quired for polarity reversal in shallow firn stacks moti-vated by the ANITA anomalous-polarity events. Third, we test the specular factorization against the full angu-lar integral. For the balloon-altitude geometry, the two agree at the 0 .2% level, while sources close to the bound-ary require the full angular integral. Section II reviews and validates the single-boundary formalism. Section III derives the stratified exten-sion. Section IV examines the specular factorization, and Sec. V presents the numerical results. Section VI discusses applications to dielectric and conducting me-dia beyond Antarctic ice. Transmission from in-medium sources, near-field source geometries, anisotropic ice, and local surface-slope structure are beyond the scope of this work.

> III. REFLECTION FROM A SPHERICAL STRATIFIED BOUNDARY

Section II gives the reflected field of a dipole above a single spherical boundary. We now develop a first-principles framework for a stratified medium beneath that boundary. The only change is the coefficient that describes the reflection of each plane wave in Eq. (5). The Sommerfeld–Weyl decomposition of the source, the tangent-plane geometry of Eq. (8), the polarization weights, and the roughness factor remain unchanged. The Fresnel coefficients of Eqs. (12)–(13) are replaced by the characteristic-matrix coefficients of the layered medium [30], obtained from the same Maxwell bound-ary conditions. 7The layered medium, or stack, consists of N homoge-neous layers beneath the boundary. The jth layer, with 

j = 1 , . . . , N , has refractive index nj and thickness dj ,and the stack lies above a semi-infinite substrate with index ns. The case N = 0 gives the single boundary con-sidered in Sec. II. The indices may be complex, so the same formalism applies to dielectric, lossy, and conduct-ing media, as demonstrated in Sec. VI for lunar regolith and seawater. The substrate represents the material be-low the structure of interest, such as deep glacial ice be-neath a firn column, seawater beneath an overlying layer, or bedrock beneath lunar regolith. Since the substrate has no lower boundary in the model, a wave transmitted into it does not return. A continuous depth profile n(z)can be approximated by a stack of sufficiently thin layers, as discussed at the end of this section. We treat the reflection of the dipole field one plane wave at a time. Each plane wave in Eq. (5) is labeled by (α, β ) and reaches the spherical boundary at an incident point, denoted by Q, Q′, and so on for different plane waves (Fig. 1). At each incident point, we introduce the plane tangent to the spherical surface (Fig. 1). Within the region around the incident point that contributes co-herently, the spherical shells can be treated as parallel layers. (8). The tangent plane and the angle α0 are different for each plane wave component because each component meets the spherical boundary at a different point (see Fig. 1). > IV. FACTORIZATION AND ITS VALIDITY LIMITS

Sections II and III evaluate the boundary coefficient in-side the angular integral, at the local incidence angle of each plane wave. Radar-sounding practice instead eval-uates it once, at the specular direction, and multiplies it onto a separately computed field. This section quantifies when that shortcut is adequate. As defined in Sec. II, the specular point is the point on the surface for which the reflected path S → Q → P is stationary. In the Sommerfeld–Weyl integral of Eq. (5), the neighborhood of this point is the stationary-phase region: the range of incident directions over which the phase changes slowly enough for the contributions to add coherently. Outside this region, rapid phase oscil-lations cancel the integral. For the balloon-borne geome-tries relevant to the ANITA/HiCal measurements and to ANITA’s successor mission PUEO [26, 27], the reflected field is dominated by plane-wave components whose inci-dent points lie close to the specular point on the spherical surface [17, 19]. The first is geometric. The phase in Eq. (5) changes rapidly with the incident direction ( α, β ), dividing the surface into Fresnel zones whose contributions alternate in sign. For this reason, we examine the layering response at individual frequencies before forming a broadband pulse or a band-averaged observable.  

> V. NUMERICAL RESULTS

All results in this section use a spherical surface with Earth’s mean radius, R = R⊕ = 6371 km, and a source height of h = 37 km, with the local incidence angle given by Eq. (8). The surface curvature is retained in the prop-agation geometry. The remaining geometric approxima-tion is the treatment of the concentric subsurface shells as parallel layers over the coherent footprint, as discussed in Sec. III and Appendix A. Results are shown for both smooth and rough sur-faces. The smooth surface sets the roughness factor of Eq. (14) to unity, while the rough surface uses the vali-dated roughness parameters of Sec. II. Reflection coeffi-cients are quoted for s polarization unless stated other-wise. In the HiCal reflection geometry, the s-polarized component gives the dominant contribution to the H-Pol response. The full H-Pol field includes both s and p com-ponents, with the azimuthal angle β entering through the weights given in Appendix B. Frequency-dependent layer ratios are computed one Fourier frequency at a time, as allowed by the linearity of the boundary problem. Broadband observables, such as band-averaged reflectivities and time-domain waveforms, are formed only after the complex field components have been evaluated across the band.  

> A. Reduction to the single-boundary limit

The first numerical check is the reduction of the lay-ered calculation to the validated single-boundary result. For the reference two-layer geometry used below, we set 

n1 = ns = 1 .35 and write the buried-layer index as 

n2 = ns + ∆ n. As ∆ n → 0, the buried interface has no index contrast and therefore produces no reflection. The layer thicknesses then become irrelevant, and the stack must reduce to a single boundary between the up-per medium and the substrate for any d1 and d2. This test checks the matrix ordering, phase convention, and normalization of the stratified calculation. Figure 3 shows the result at 15 ◦ elevation; the same test was also performed at other elevation angles. As the buried-layer contrast decreases, the frequency-dependent layering fringes disappear. At ∆ n = 0, the layered and single-boundary calculations agree to machine precision, with a largest fractional deviation of 9 .99 × 10 −16 across the band. Repeating the test at the ten validation eleva-tions in Table I gives the same agreement, with a largest fractional deviation of 1 .22 × 10 −15 over the full scan.

> C. A polarity-reversal threshold for buried layers

Polarity reversal of the reflected pulse, relative to the single-boundary inversion, requires the total reflected field from the layered boundary to change sign. That field is the coherent sum of the reflection from the up-per surface and the contributions that enter the stack, reflect from buried interfaces, and re-emerge into the up-per medium. The sum depends on both the amplitudes of these contributions and their relative radio-frequency phase, which is set by the optical path length through the layers at the local incidence angle. The threshold there-fore depends on geometry. Scanning the index n2 of the middle layer in the reference stack shows this dependence directly (Fig. 5). For the s-polarized coefficient over 150– 850 MHz, the first crossing occurs at n2 ≃ 2.56, 2 .04, and 1.79 for elevations 8 ◦, 15 ◦, and 25 ◦, respectively. The threshold is therefore not a universal material number. The realistic firn contrast tested here, n2 =1.75, stays below threshold at all three elevations. Dense ice, n ≃ 1.78, is below threshold at 8 ◦ and 15 ◦ by wide margins, but at 25 ◦ the crossing is at 1 .79, so a buried dense-ice layer sits essentially at threshold. The margin, about 0 .01 in refractive index, is smaller than the spread in reported dense-ice values. A strongly reflecting buried layer such as water or brine lies far above these dielectric thresholds at any elevation and would leave a distinctive polarity signature relevant to planetary and subglacial sounding. The thresholds quoted above are deterministic out-puts of a forward calculation rather than measurements, so they carry no statistical uncertainty. What they do carry is a dependence on the assumed stack, and it is worth quantifying which inputs matter for this calcula-tion. Three were varied at 15 ◦ elevation, where the ref-erence value is nth 2 = 2 .04. The layer thicknesses matter least. Varying the equal layer thicknesses over d1 = d2 = 1, 2, 3, 4, and 8 m shifts the threshold between 2 .16 and 2 .04, and unequal thicknesses in the range 1–4 m change it by less than 0 .04. The reason is that the threshold is set by the sign of the coherent sum somewhere in the band, and once a layer is thick enough to contribute several Airy fringes across the test frequency band 150–850 MHz, adding more fringes does not change whether a sign change occurs. Only for sub-meter layers, which contribute less than one fringe across the band, does the threshold begin to rise. The upper-layer index matters most. Taking n1 = ns

A detailed treat-ment of grazing incidence and skin-depth effects requires a separate study. Bright reflections from beneath the Martian south po-lar layered deposits have been interpreted as possible ev-idence for basal liquid water, but they can also arise from constructive interference between thin, closely spaced layers [45, 46]. The frequency dependence of the reflected power can help distinguish these cases. In the present formalism, this dependence is described by the layering ratio Q σ (f ) of Eq. (32). Its frequency scale is set by the layer thickness, so measurements at only a few frequen-cies may not resolve the interference pattern. A similar issue arises for icy moons. REASON on Europa Clip-per operates at 9 and 60 MHz, while RIME on JUICE operates at 9 MHz. Both instruments must distinguish reflections from layered ice, including possible salt-rich layers, from reflections associated with deeper ice–ocean interfaces [33, 34]. These cases also probe the limits of the specular ap-proximation, as shown in Table II. For orbital sounders, the region contributing coherently is typically much wider than in the balloon geometry, and the locally pla-nar approximation becomes less accurate for smaller bod-ies. The difference becomes especially important for a source only a few meters above the surface. In that case, the coherent region can span tens of degrees, the stationary-phase approximation used in Eq. (36) breaks down, and the full angular integral is needed. This regime is relevant to future lander or rover bistatic measure-ments, where the reflected-to-direct field ratio could be used to study shallow subsurface structure. Plane-wave layered models are already widely used in radar sounding. The exact angular calculation developed here provides a way to test these approximations when the source is close to the surface. The same formalism can be applied to other layered media by changing the stack and its refrac-tive indices. Examples include layered ice shells of ocean worlds [32], orbital sounding of embedded dielectric lay-ers, and lunar reflection problems. Salt formations, which have been considered as radio-transparent media for neu-trino detection [49], and desert sand enter through their dielectric properties in the same way. The lunar case is particularly relevant to global 21-cm cosmology. The Moon contributes to the measured sky spectrum through the antenna backlobe, with both thermal emission from the regolith and reflected Galac-tic emission. Current analyses treat lunar reflectivity as a free parameter in the analysis [43]. A layered-boundary calculation of the type developed here could instead pre-dict the reflectivity and its frequency dependence from an assumed regolith structure, providing an input to analy-ses such as PRATUSH [44].

This extends the single-boundary spherical-wave treatment of Ref. [17] and the local plane-wave treatment of curved surfaces in Ref. [19] to stratified media. The calculation has three distinct levels of scope. The results demonstrated in this paper include a spherical external geometry, a stratified medium with real or com-plex refractive index, reflection, exact angular integra-tion, and validation against both the HiCal-2 reflectivity results and the HiCal-1 pulse pairs. The same framework can also treat spatially varying stratification, transmis-sion, conducting media, and near-surface bistatic geome-tries. The planetary examples in Sec. VI use the valid-ity criteria derived in Sec. IV rather than full sounding simulations. Fully curved multilayer scattering, lateral variations within the coherent footprint, and inversion of measured data are beyond the scope of the present work. The forward calculation is validated at several levels. It agrees with the published band-averaged single-boundary result to within 1 .1% at ten elevation angles and ap-proaches that result at machine precision when the layer contrast is removed. It also reproduces the measured HiCal-1 reflected pulses, with a best-pair signed correla-tion of 0 .83 and the expected polarity inversion in 95% of the 106 matched HiCal-1 direct/reflected pulse pairs. Applied to the stratified polar-firn model, the calcula-tion gives a clear result. For the reference stack, the layering ratio varies from 0 .14 to 1 .68 in amplitude over 150–850 MHz, while the phase shift remains below about 

±65 ◦. The reflected field does not reverse polarity for the firn contrast considered here. The buried index re-quired for a sign change depends strongly on elevation, with thresholds of n2 ≃ 2.56, 2 .04, and 1 .79 at 8 ◦, 15 ◦,and 25 ◦ elevation, respectively. 18 This result is directly relevant to the anomalous-polarity events reported by ANITA. Ref. [28] proposed shallow subsurface firn layering and density inversions as a possible way to produce a reflected pulse with the same polarity as a direct pulse. Ref. [29] tested this idea against ANITA and HiCal reflectivity data and found it disfavored at the surveyed locations. The present calcu-lation provides a complementary quantitative result. For a specified geometry and shallow two-layer stack, it de-termines how large the buried refractive-index contrast must be before the reflection coefficient changes sign. The realistic firn contrast remains below this threshold over the elevations studied here. This supports the con-clusion of Ref. [29] and places a quantitative constraint on the shallow-layering mechanism proposed in Ref. [28]. The constraint is strongest near the horizon, where the required index becomes much larger than any realistic firn or ice value. At higher elevation, dense-ice-like con-trasts approach the threshold. The result is therefore best stated as an elevation-dependent constraint rather than as a single material threshold. The same calculation can be applied directly to the reported ANITA event geometries. For the two steep ANITA-I/III anomalous-polarity events, the reported payload elevation angles are −27 .4◦ and −35 .0◦. The cor-responding surface-emergence angles used in tau-lepton interpretations are 25 .4◦ and 35 .5◦, with an uncertainty of about 1 ◦ [12, 13, 50]. These are the relevant local sur-face angles derived from the spherical geometry used in the present calculation. They give sign-change thresholds of nth 2 ≃ 1.78 and 1 .68, respectively. The four ANITA-IV near-horizon events require a dif-ferent conversion because the reported quantities are off-sets below the payload radio horizon, θ − θH = −0.19 ◦

to −0.81 ◦ [14, 51]. Using the same spherical-Earth ge-ometry as for the HiCal benchmark, with R = R⊕ and 

h = 37 km, these correspond to local surface elevations of 1.54 ◦, 1 .78 ◦, 2 .91 ◦, and 3 .27 ◦. The corresponding thresh-olds are nth 2 ≃ 5.41, 5 .04, 4 .04, and 3 .81, respectively (Fig. 9). The uncertainty in the event angle has a much larger ef-fect near the horizon. For the steep ANITA-I/III events, changing the elevation by ±1◦ changes the threshold by only about 0 .02. For the near-horizon ANITA-IV events, the same angular uncertainty gives ranges of roughly 4 .2– 9.0 for the shallowest event and 3 .4–4 .5 for the deepest event. The central values should therefore not be inter-preted to three significant figures. The main conclusion is unchanged because even the full uncertainty ranges remain well above realistic firn and ice indices. We also applied the frequency-dependent reference-stack transfer function to the 106 measured HiCal-1 di-rect H-Pol pulses at these six event-angle values. None of the calculated reflected templates changed to the non-inverted polarity at any of the angles. The result was 0/106 at each angle and 0 /636 over all six tests. The same result was obtained when both the validated and alternative positive roughness weights were included in the spectral stress test. Thus, within the tested shallow and laterally uniform stack, the frequency-dependent lay-ering does not reproduce the non-inverted polarity seen in the ANITA events. There are two important limits to this conclusion. The first concerns surface structure rather than subsurface layering. The calculation assumes a laterally uniform stack. Sastrugi, wind crusts, and larger-scale surface slopes affect the reflection through the surface geome-try and roughness, which is a separate part of the prob-lem. Ref. [18] studied these effects using surface features drawn from REMA elevation data [36]. That work found that anisotropic roughness can change the reflected wave-form and its amplitude while retaining the usual polarity inversion, whereas local surface structure can produce non-inverted reflected pulses for some geometries. The present result therefore constrains the shallow-layering explanation without closing the question of surface to-pography. The second limit is that a reflection-coefficient thresh-old does not explain any individual ANITA event. We do not identify the origin of the anomalous-polarity events here. Rather, the result removes a specific proposed mechanism from the range of shallow-layer parameters tested, using a forward model that has been indepen-dently checked against measured reflection data. This constraint is part of a broader picture. A dedi-cated search for upward-going air showers with the Pierre Auger Observatory Fluorescence Detector found no such events. If the ANITA anomalous-polarity event rate were produced by a persistent flux of genuine upward-going showers, the reported rate would predict several to sev-eral dozen events depending on the assumed spectral in-dex [52]. This places additional constraints on interpreta-tions based on upward-going particles. The present work provides a corresponding quantitative constraint on one of the proposed surface-reflection explanations. The validity analysis in Sec. IV also shows where the full calculation is most useful. For a balloon-altitude source, the specular approximation agrees with the ex-act angular calculation at the 0 .2% level, so the faster specular treatment is sufficient for HiCal-like geometries. The situation changes as the source approaches the sur-face. The stationary-phase region becomes wider, and the reflection coefficient can vary significantly across it. This is relevant for in-medium sources such as an in-ice cascade close to the surface, an in-regolith source near a buried layer, or a shallow calibration transmitter. This matters because surface reflectivity enters directly into the reconstruction of air-shower energy. It is the quantity that HiCal was flown to measure [17]. An error in the reflection amplitude therefore propagates into the reconstructed field strength and inferred pri-mary energy, and can also affect the detection efficiency for direct/reflected pairs near threshold. Arrival-time-based vertex reconstruction is not affected in the same way. In-ice cosmic-ray and neutrino measurements [25] provide another example of this regime. Cosmic-ray-19 induced Askaryan emission produced close to the ice sur-face is a transmission problem at source heights where the stationary-phase approximation begins to break down. These cases motivate a dedicated treatment of near-field transmission. Several extensions follow naturally from the present work. The roughness model used here is the validated isotropic factor of Ref. [17]. Anisotropic roughness and larger-scale surface structure can be incorporated using the local-slope treatment developed in Ref. [18]. The conducting-media results in Sec. VI demonstrate the range of the formalism, but grazing incidence and skin-depth effects require further validation. The polarity sig-nature of buried water or brine is also directly relevant to planetary and subglacial sounding, where the sign of a re-flected pulse can help distinguish a conducting layer from a dielectric one. Finally, combining the local plane-wave treatment of Ref. [19] with the layered coefficient devel-oped here would allow calibration-pulser data at other in-cidence angles to provide an additional test of the model. A further application is the inverse problem. The cal-culation developed here is a forward model. Given a set of layer indices and thicknesses, it predicts the reflected field. A sounding experiment poses the reverse question of recovering nj , d j from the measured waveform. Dif-ferent stacks can produce similar responses over a lim-ited frequency band, so this inversion can be degener-ate. An accurate and efficient forward model is there-fore an essential starting point for fitting or sampling the layer parameters, whether directly or through a surro-gate model trained on synthetic data. The layer-versus-water problem discussed in Sec. VI is a natural exam-ple. The present work provides the forward calculation needed to move from comparisons of individual model stacks toward quantitative inference from measured re-flected waveforms.  

> VIII. CONCLUSION

We have developed a wave-optics forward model for spherical-wave reflection from a spherical boundary with a stratified medium beneath it. The model combines the Sommerfeld–Weyl representation of the source field with a characteristic-matrix calculation of the layered reflec-tion coefficient. Each plane-wave component is evaluated at the local incidence angle where it meets the surface, and the reflected field is obtained by coherent integration over the full angular spectrum. The single-boundary limit reproduces the published spherical-surface reflectivity to better than 1 .1% at ten elevation angles and approaches the same result at ma-chine precision when the layer contrast is removed. The calculation also reproduces the measured HiCal-1 re-flected pulses, with a best-pair signed correlation of 0 .83. Across 106 matched direct/reflected pairs, 95% favor the expected polarity inversion. For the stratified polar-firn model considered here, the buried layers produce substantial frequency-dependent changes in the reflected amplitude, with |Q| ranging from 0.14 to 1 .68 over 150–850 MHz. The corresponding phase shift remains below about ±65 ◦, but the reflected field does not reverse polarity for the realistic firn contrast tested. The refractive index required for a sign change depends strongly on elevation, with n2 ≃ 2.56, 2 .04, and 1 .79 at 8 ◦, 15 ◦, and 25 ◦, respectively. These re-sults place a quantitative constraint on shallow subsur-face layering as an explanation of the anomalous-polarity ANITA events. They also show how the same calculation can distinguish dielectric layers from strongly reflecting buried interfaces in planetary and subglacial sounding. The exact angular calculation also provides a di-rect test of the usual specular approximation. For the balloon-altitude benchmark, the specular factorization agrees with the full calculation to 0 .2%. As the source approaches the surface, however, the stationary-phase re-gion becomes wider and the local reflection coefficient can no longer be treated as constant across it. The full an-gular calculation is therefore important for near-surface sources, where using a specular model could attribute a geometrical effect to the subsurface structure. The formalism developed in this work is not restricted to ice. The boundary enters through the complex re-fractive index, allowing the same treatment to describe materials ranging from firn and ice to lunar regolith and conducting media. The model retains the full spherical geometry and coherent wave effects that are absent from standard plane-wave treatments. It provides a common forward framework for interpreting reflected radio signals from stratified natural media and a basis for future mea-surements aimed at recovering subsurface structure from the amplitude, phase, and polarity of the reflected signal.

> 8. Approximations

The main elements of the calculation are exact. The Sommerfeld–Weyl decomposition of Eq. (5) is an identity, not an expansion. The transverse projection of Eq. (B3) and the reflection of each plane wave from the local tan-gent plane follow from the boundary conditions. The layered reflection coefficient replaces the single-boundary coefficient as the solution of the same boundary-value problem. These steps do not require weak layer contrast, thin layers, or a monotonic refractive-index profile. The approximations enter in treating the curved shells as parallel to the local tangent plane, with the resulting phase error bounded by Eq. (A8), and in neglecting the evanescent part of the contour in Eq. (5), which is negli-gible for the far-field geometry considered here. For lat-erally varying layers, the structure must also vary slowly across the coherent footprint. Thus, the stratified calculation retains the same geo-metric approximation as the single-boundary calculation; only the boundary coefficient is replaced. The single-boundary validation in Sec. II therefore also tests this part of the construction.

7. Scope of the approximation 

Within the local tangent plane, the layered calculation solves Maxwell’s equations exactly, including the fields in each layer, the boundary conditions at every inter-face, and all internal reflections. The Sommerfeld–Weyl decomposition is also exact. The only approximation is geometric. In the transverse direction, the concentric shells are treated as locally parallel layers, as described in Appendix A. The resulting phase error is bounded by Eq. (A8), giv-ing ∆ ϕ = πh ′/R ≤ πh/R ≃ 0.018 rad for the balloon ge-ometry. This is the same geometric approximation used in the single-boundary calculation of Refs. [17, 19]. Intro-ducing stratification does not add a new approximation.
